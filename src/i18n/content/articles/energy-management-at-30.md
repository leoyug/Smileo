title: "Managing energy at 30"
description: "Noticing exhausting evenings, putting waiting tasks in their place, and asking one more question before taking on a new request."
category: "Life"
---
One evening recently, I was still sitting at the dining table after dinner and the dishes were done, mentally rewriting a sentence I had sent a colleague that afternoon. I worried they might have misunderstood. There were no new messages, but I reopened the conversation several times. By the time I remembered to shower, another half hour had gone.

A record of that day would probably say nothing more than a normal day at work.

It felt unfair.

After turning 30, I started noticing these evenings: nothing major had happened, yet even choosing a film felt like effort. I used to assess a day by how much I finished. Now I also ask how long I keep living inside a task after it ends. These are a few recent adjustments, tested on a sample of one, mainly for people who continue working in their heads after work.

## Write down what is waiting

Unanswered things easily hold my attention. I send a document, hear nothing and start wondering whether it was worded badly. I reopen it, change a few words and eventually become unsure which version I sent. Nobody asked me to keep working.

I tried listing waiting items separately with a time for the next action. If I will ask again on Thursday, there is no action to complete on Tuesday evening unless a new question actually arrives. I write that one line without analyzing why there has been no reply.

It does not move a project forward automatically, but it clarifies what I can do now. For someone who repeatedly examines other people's tone, knowing there is no next step tonight is more concrete than telling myself to relax.

The cost is tolerating a little uncertainty. At first, I kept wanting to check early. Apparently I even need practice at waiting as agreed, which is a little funny.

## Tiredness depends on the task

I used to declare the whole evening a loss as soon as I felt tired, then scroll until my eyes hurt and blame myself for wasting what remained. An extra round of guilt made the package complete.

Now I ask what I still feel willing to do. Sometimes writing has become frustrating, but repotting balcony plants sounds pleasant. After a day of coordinating people, even replying to what time tomorrow feels like too much, while organizing a drawer alone feels manageable.

These activities do not necessarily leave me refreshed. I have simply stopped doing the kind of work I was resisting. On evenings when I need solitude, arranging conversation is especially demanding, even with a friend I like very much.

I do not intend to classify every form of fatigue. If I want to do nothing, I can shower, go to bed and reconsider tomorrow. Being tired should not require a fatigue-analysis report as well.

## Give the ending a clear handoff

The hardest habit to change is agreeing to things just before bed. Someone asks whether I can look at something, I say yes, then discover dozens of pages attached. The last half hour of the evening and the first task of tomorrow are booked, and I feel awkward withdrawing.

Now I ask about the deadline first and confirm when I can look. Some conversations reveal that nothing needs doing tonight at all; the urgency was my own eagerness. One extra question before agreeing is easier than repeatedly thinking about it afterward.

I also leave a closing line for current work: sent, awaiting confirmation, or stopped at page eight, continue tomorrow morning. That makes the next starting point clear without keeping the task in mind all evening to prove I am responsible.

These practices assume I can negotiate response times and currently have no duty requiring me to get up in the night to care for someone. Different circumstances would need different adjustments. Thirty-five was simply the age when I began paying attention to these things; using it to explain all tiredness would be too easy.

Next week, I will try accepting one fewer last-minute request and see whether it creates an evening without tomorrow hanging over it. For now, this is enough writing. The bag of soil on the balcony is still open; I will go close it.
